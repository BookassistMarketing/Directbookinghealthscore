## The Gap Is Now Measurable

The 2026 European Accommodation Barometer, published by Booking.com from a survey of 1,240 accommodation executives across 24 European markets with fieldwork between 5 February and 24 March 2026, found broad optimism across the sector. It also found something more useful: a consistent, measurable gap between what chains report and what independents report.

| Indicator | Chains | Independents |
|---|---|---|
| Rate their economic situation positively | 72% | 55% |
| Observed room rate increases in the last six months | 47% | 37% |
| Observed occupancy increases | 56% | 46% |

The pattern repeats on capability. On cybersecurity preparedness, 94 per cent of large properties with 250 or more employees feel prepared, against 60 per cent of properties with fewer than ten staff. Cybersecurity is a top concern for 21 per cent of smaller businesses but only 9 per cent of large properties. On staff training, 89 per cent of larger employers offer it, compared with less than half of small operators at 49 per cent.

Around 70 per cent of European hotels are small independents. This is not a niche finding about a handful of properties. It is the shape of the market.

## Why the Gap Widens by Itself

Nothing in these numbers suggests independents are less ambitious or less commercially aware. The mechanism is simpler and harsher. A chain has specialists. It has a revenue team, a distribution team, a digital team and a compliance function. An independent has a general manager who is also the revenue manager, the marketing department and the person handling a broken lift.

That difference compounds. Every new obligation and every new channel, whether AI visibility, agentic booking rails, data protection or sustainability claims rules, costs a chain a project and costs an independent a weekend. When capacity is the constraint, the correct response is not to work harder across every front. It is to do fewer things properly.

## The Three Things That Actually Move an Independent's Numbers

If you have limited hours, spend them here, in this order.

### One: fix the direct conversion path

Your website and booking engine are the only part of the funnel you fully control, and they are where the highest return per hour of attention sits. Time your own booking flow on a phone. If it is slow, if the rate display is confusing, or if reaching the confirmation screen takes more than a couple of minutes, you are paying commission for traffic you already had.

### Two: capture consented guest data every single stay

A chain wins repeat business with a loyalty programme. An independent can win it with a clean email list and a reason to use it. Capture consent at booking, at check in and after the stay, keep the data tidy, and use it two or three times a year with something genuinely worth opening. This is the cheapest competitive advantage available to a small property.

### Three: keep your information accurate everywhere it appears

Your rates, room descriptions, facilities and photography need to be consistent across your own site, your OTA listings, your Google presence and the directories that quote you. Inconsistency costs you in search rankings, in AI recommendations and in guest trust. Correcting it costs nothing but a methodical afternoon.

Everything else, from a new PMS integration to a rebrand, can wait until those three are solid. The reason independents lose ground is rarely that they missed a trend. It is that the fundamentals were never finished, which is the same underlying pattern behind [why hotels lose money to OTAs](/blog/why-hotels-lose-money-to-otas).
